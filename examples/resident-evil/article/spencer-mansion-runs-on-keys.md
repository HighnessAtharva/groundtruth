# The Spencer Mansion Runs on Keys

Survival horror sold itself on the zombies. Open the 1996 original again and the zombies are the least of it. The mansion is a warehouse management problem wearing a horror costume, and almost every moment of tension in it comes from a slot you do not have rather than a monster you cannot kill.

## The number that decides everything

Jill carries eight inventory slots and Chris carries six. Nothing in the game changes either number. A key takes one slot, exactly like a shotgun or a herb, which means every key you pick up is a weapon you are not carrying.

That is the whole design, and the mansion is built to press on it. There are more locked doors on the first floor than a single character can open in one pass while still carrying a weapon, ammunition and something to heal with. You do not run out of keys. You run out of hands.

## The keys are not the puzzle, the trips are

Count the doors and the shape becomes obvious. The Sword Key opens six doors. The Armor Key opens three doors. The Shield Key opens three, two of them upstairs. Between them those three keys cover most of the mansion, and none of them can be thrown away until the last door it opens is behind you.

So the mansion turns into a routing problem. You leave the item box holding four things you will need in twenty minutes and walk back for them, and the walk is where the game happens. The famous backtracking is not padding. It is the direct consequence of a six-slot bag, and it disappears the moment you play Jill with the lockpick, because the lockpick opens every Small Key drawer and is never consumed. One item replaces four.

## The save resource does the same job

Ink Ribbons are the only save resource in the mansion, and they take a slot too. A player carrying two ribbons is a player carrying one fewer key. The typewriter in the main hall is close to everything, the one in the study is the furthest point on the second floor, and choosing which to walk to is another slot decision wearing a different hat.

None of this reads as accounting while you play it. It reads as dread. The reason a locked door in this game lands harder than a locked door in almost any game since is that you already know what opening it will cost you, and the cost is measured in the things you had to leave behind.

## What the remake softened

The 2002 remake widened the bag and added the defensive items, and both changes are improvements to how the game feels minute to minute. Together they cost the mansion its best pressure. When the bag is big enough to carry every key at once, the door graph stops being a graph. It becomes a checklist.

Most players finish a first run in about nine hours, and a good share of those hours are walking. That walking is the game. Take it out and you have a shooter with a fixed camera.

Shinji Mikami directed the 1996 original, and the interviews from the period talk about the fixed camera and the tank controls as the sources of tension. Twenty-nine years later the camera reads as a period style choice. The six-slot bag still reads as design.
